I write about software most of the time, but two other things shape my days just as much: **type 1 diabetes** and **running**. They're more connected than they first look — both are feedback systems where a small input now changes an outcome an hour from now.

## Exercise is an insulin multiplier

The thing nobody tells you early on: moderate aerobic exercise makes your body far more sensitive to whatever insulin is already on board. A dose that's perfectly fine at rest can send you low mid-run. So the plan starts *before* the run, not during it.

## What actually works for me

1. **Check the trend, not just the number.** A flat 6.5 mmol/L is a green light; a 6.5 falling fast is not.
2. **Reduce basal ahead of time.** For anything over 30 minutes I lower basal ~60–90 minutes before starting.
3. **Carry fast carbs.** Gels win over solid food — they act quickly when you need them.
4. **Log everything, review weekly.** This is the part that feels like engineering.

## Treating it like a system

The weekly review is where the coding brain helps. I keep a tiny table of *run type → pre-run adjustment → outcome* and adjust one variable at a time, the same way I'd bisect a bug.

| Run type | Basal change | Pre-run carbs | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy 5k | −30% | none | stable |
| Long run | −60% | 20g | slight rise, then flat |
| Intervals | none | 10g | tends to spike |

Intervals are the interesting outlier — short, intense efforts can *raise* blood sugar via adrenaline, the opposite of a steady run. Same body, different input, opposite result. That's the whole game.

## Why I write about it here

If you're a developer who also manages a condition like this, the mindset transfers both directions: the patience you learn debugging a flaky system is the same patience that keeps you calm when the numbers don't cooperate. More of these to come under **Life**.
